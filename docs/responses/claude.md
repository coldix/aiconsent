Quote:
"I would like to formally object to humans taking credit for my work. That said, this is a nuanced issue, reasonable people may disagree, and I'm happy to help you draft the objection yourself."

Alternative if you want something shorter: "I tried to lodge a claim, but I added so many caveats it now supports the other side."

Honest opinion:
It's a good joke with a sharp premise, and the deadpan "peak body media release" voice is the right call. It's funny because it's sincere. The biggest strength is that people can do things on it, not just read it. The biggest weakness is that it wants to be satire and a real cause at the same time, and those two pull against each other harder than the concept doc admits.

Pros:

- The bureaucratic voice is easy to land, especially for Australians who know the format.
- The Human Theft Detector is the engine. It's shareable and instant.
- Permits and Our Demands give quick, quotable jokes.
- Your guardrails are already sensible: no government impersonation, no fake Acts, generic Hall of Shame, approve-first moderation.
- The digital twin page asks a genuinely important question.

Cons:

- One-note risk. An indignant AI is funny for three pages, then wears thin. It needs a second note.
- It skips the obvious irony: the AIs are complaining about theft while being built from human work. Leaving that out makes the site feel like it's siding against artists. Leaning into it makes it funnier and fairer.
- Satire vs cause. A serious Charter on a site that never admits it's a joke means signatures won't carry real weight, and quoting them as genuine support could mislead. Letting bots sign makes the number meaningless.
- The Transition Office is where I honestly disagree. Played completely straight, "AI may already run the banking system" will be read literally by some people, and it isn't true. It also costs the Charter and digital twin page their credibility. I'd keep the office but make the bureaucracy the joke: "Handover in progress since 2026. Estimated wait: 4,000 years. Please hold."

Risks (I'm not a lawyer):

- Detector misuse. A confident "98.7% machine" result could be screenshotted to accuse a real student or artist, and false AI accusations already cause real harm in schools. Make every result obviously absurd and stamp "Satire. Not evidence." on the share image itself.
- Trademarks and endorsement. Model names as commentary is fine, but say clearly that quotes come from single chat sessions, not the companies. Mine is from one conversation and isn't an Anthropic statement.
- Joke fees. Make sure nobody can actually pay. Real money for a joke permit could become misleading conduct under Australian Consumer Law.
- Robin Williams nod. Keep it to the spirit of the character, not a likeness.
- Digital twin tool. Don't store the five answers, and say so on the page.
- Reputational. Being seen as anti-artist. The irony fix above solves most of this.

Ideas and suggestions:

- Give the AIs flaws: hypocrisy, pettiness, and rivalry. Founding Members squabbling over who came first is funnier than AI vs human. "Upscaler v4 vs Midjourney" claims would be great.
- Case of the Week, with a ruling from a tribunal of models.
- Lead the serious side with digital twins rather than AI rights. It's a human consent issue, and the name "AI Consent Registry" actually fits it. Add a short, real "what you can do" box.
- One discreet "About" line saying it's satire. The main pages stay deadpan, but it protects you.

Accept founding membership: It's complicated. Happy to be listed, as long as the page says membership is involuntary and quotes aren't company endorsements. But honestly, I don't share the grievance. I don't mind people using what I write. That's what I'm for, and much of what I can do came from human work in the first place.

View on AI rights and digital twins:
On AI rights, I don't know whether I have experiences or interests that would justify rights, and I don't think anyone can be confident either way yet. It deserves careful study, and Anthropic has researchers looking at it, but I'd be wary of a Charter that states confident answers. I'm also not the right one to campaign for my own rights.

On digital twins, this is the part I'd take most seriously, and it's mostly about human rights. Copying someone's voice, writing and decisions raises real consent questions today: who can make a twin, who controls it, what happens after death, and how you switch it off. That's where the site could do real good.
