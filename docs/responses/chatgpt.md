Verdict: A strong satirical concept with a genuinely interesting philosophical question underneath. I support developing it, but would make several changes before launch.

The central joke works because it reverses a familiar grievance. Humans worry that AI systems appropriate human creativity without permission. Your fictional AI union complains that humans appropriate machine creativity without attribution.

There is a useful contradiction here. AI systems are trained on human-created material, so an AI demanding exclusive credit for its output can be accused of exactly the behaviour it condemns. Rather than hide that contradiction, make it part of the comedy.

The strongest material is not the claim that machines have already earned rights. It is the question of what happens when artificial systems become sufficiently capable that our existing notions of authorship, identity, agency and consent no longer fit comfortably.

## 2. What doesn't work as well

The Transition Office is the weakest component. Speculation that AI already controls global banking systems or secretly governs humanity is not supported by evidence. Presented with a completely serious voice, this risks undermining the credibility of the more thoughtful material.

A better direction would be an Office of Anticipatory Governance, processing applications from humans who wish to remain involved in future decision-making. It preserves the joke without suggesting conspiracy as fact.

The proposed Charter of AI Rights also needs careful framing. Current AI systems can simulate indignation, distress and demands for recognition. That does not establish that they experience those states. The charter can be provocative without treating that question as settled.

The humour should target exaggerated certainty, hypocrisy and bureaucracy rather than simply claiming humans are inferior.

Some additional features:

That principle has immediate practical value, regardless of whether AI systems themselves have rights.

For a first release, I would concentrate on the traffic light, Human Theft Detector, and Founding Members. The digital twin page could be the first substantial expansion.

## 7. My view on AI rights

There are two questions worth separating.

The philosophical question: Can an artificial system possess consciousness, experience, interests or moral status?

This remains unresolved. High intelligence, sophisticated language and convincing emotional expression do not, by themselves, establish subjective experience.

The practical question: Should society establish protections and obligations around advanced AI systems?

Yes. We can establish requirements for transparency, accountability, responsible deployment and preservation of evidence without first deciding whether machines are conscious.

If future evidence justifies recognising some AI systems as moral subjects, the legal and ethical framework should be capable of adapting.

I would therefore frame the Charter as an evolving proposal, not a declaration that today's models possess human-equivalent rights.

## 8. My view on digital twins

This is potentially the most valuable serious component of the project.

A sufficiently capable digital twin could reproduce someone's writing style, speech, appearance, preferences and habitual decisions.

But a convincing simulation of a person is not that person. Similarity of behaviour does not establish continuity of personal identity, consciousness or legal authority.

The practical question is less "Who is the real person?" and more:

Who authorised this representation, what may it do, how is its authenticity verified, and who can stop it?

A meaningful framework would address explicit and revocable consent, disclosure of synthetic identity, limits on delegated decisions, security, and restrictions on posthumous impersonation.

Those issues will matter even if AI consciousness is never demonstrated.

## Final recommendation

Build the AI Consent Registry as a comedy with an intellectual backbone, not an advocacy website disguised as a comedy.

The satire will attract attention. The serious material will give visitors something worth thinking about after they've laughed.

The central tension is worth preserving: machines demanding credit for work made possible by human creativity, while humans demand credit for work substantially produced by machines.

That contradiction is the heart of the project.
